## Item

**`fee(a)` on a pair errs in BOTH directions, and the spec's worked example only shows the safe one**

## Spec ref

03 §5.3a(2a); `app/packages/protocol/src/redemption.ts`; `reference-model/fixtures/vectors.json` `ledger_fee_scenarios`

## Status

**measured 2026-08-06**

## Result

§5.3a(2a) rejects `fee(a)` on the pair calls with a worked witness at `a = 20,000`, `s = 0.70005`, 30 bps: `fee(a) = 60` against `fee_pair = 43`, because the SHORT leg falls under `min_split` and the waiver exempts it. I wrote the differential asserting that direction — that the combined base always takes **more** — and the corpus refuted it on its first run. At `a = 1,000,000`, same `s`, same rate: legs of 700,050 and 299,950 charge `ceil(2100.15) + ceil(899.85) = 2101 + 900 = 3001`, while `fee(a) = ceil(3000.0) = 3000`. **Two independent ceilings can exceed one**, so the shortcut *understates* wherever no leg is waived and the flooring loses nothing. That is the direction a client must fear and the one the spec's example does not show: a displayed fee below the chain's puts a `net` **above** what the account receives, on the screen 11 §11.5 rule 3 makes the deciding figure. The port was already correct — it computes from the legs — but the *test* would have passed an implementation that used the combined base whenever the two happened to agree, and the assertion I had written would have failed for the right reason with the wrong explanation. It now requires **both** directions to appear in the corpus, which no single-direction implementation can satisfy. The general lesson is about worked examples: a spec that demonstrates a rule with one witness has shown the rule, not the error surface, and a differential written from the witness inherits its blind side.
